What the list looks like

food-waste-city · seed 11 · first eight ideas
straight
  1. Require grocery stores to donate safe unsold food to food banks and community groups through scheduled daily pickups
  2. Adopt a citywide "use-by, not best-by" labeling standard to reduce confusion-driven disposal of safe food
  3. Offer curbside collection of organic waste from households and businesses, with materials sent to composting or anaerobic digestion facilities
  4. Run a public education campaign showing households that small purchasing changes can save hundreds of dollars annually on wasted groceries
  5. Provide small food businesses with inventory-tracking templates and training so they can forecast demand more accurately and order less
  6. Build a shared commercial kitchen where restaurants, bakeries, and institutions can lawfully transform surplus ingredients into new products
  7. Encourage restaurants to offer half portions, smaller dish sizes, and à la carte options so customers can order only what they will eat
  8. Require large venues, festivals, and catering events to donate leftover food rather than send it to disposal
daydream in the middle
  1. Require large supermarkets to donate edible surplus to food banks before it reaches the waste stream, with tax relief as the incentive
  2. Create a citywide surplus-rescue marketplace where stores and kitchens list discounted end-of-day inventory in real time
  3. Fund "rescue boxes" on every neighborhood collection route so drivers can drop unsold food at community fridges rather than bins
  4. Build a shared cold-storage network so small grocers and co-ops can hold excess stock cheaply instead of discarding it
  5. Grant restaurants a legal safe-harbor to donate prepared food without fear of liability, provided it goes to vetted charities
  6. Introduce a "use it up" label on a shelf-stable goods so consumers see which products are most at risk of being wasted
  7. Publish a live city food-waste dashboard so residents, businesses, and officials can see where waste concentrates by neighborhood and season
  8. Standardize a single donation form and pickup protocol across charities so businesses spend minutes, not hours, arranging transfers

What the list looks like

food-waste-city · seed 11 · first eight ideas
straight
  1. Create a citywide food waste audit to identify the biggest sources of waste
  2. Launch a public education campaign on meal planning and proper food storage
  3. Offer free workshops on cooking with leftovers and imperfect produce
  4. Establish a municipal composting program for households and businesses
  5. Partner with grocery stores to discount soon-to-expire foods
  6. Set up a food rescue network to redistribute surplus food to shelters
  7. Require large food businesses to report their food waste annually
  8. Provide tax incentives to restaurants that reduce food waste
daydream in the middle
  1. A citywide food-waste audit could identify which neighborhoods, businesses, and institutions generate the most preventable surplus
  2. Municipal procurement rules could require caterers and city-run cafeterias to report and reduce edible surplus
  3. A shared cold-storage hub could let small grocers and restaurants hold near-expiry food safely before redistribution
  4. A standardized “surplus shelf” program could give participating stores clear signage and liability guidance for discounted near-date items
  5. Local bakeries could partner with evening transit routes to move unsold bread to shelters, community fridges, or discount markets
  6. A digital surplus exchange could connect restaurants and grocers with volunteers, food banks, and composters in real time
  7. Neighborhood composting cooperatives could turn unavoidable food scraps into soil amendments for community gardens
  8. City inspections could reward businesses that track waste, donate surplus, or use small-portion options
