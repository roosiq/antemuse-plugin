dev-lighthouse-door
before — baseline

The keeper, a man named Aldric who had tended the light for thirty-one years without once finding anything new in the rock, knelt and pressed his palm flat against the door. It was warm. Not sun-warmed—something deeper, something that pulsed faintly against his lifeline like a second heartbeat. There was no handle, no keyhole, only a seam of pale light that traced the outline and widened by a hair when he leaned close enough to hear the sea moving on the other side. Except the sea, he realized with a stillness creeping up his spine, was not on the other side of this wall. The sea was forty feet to the east, and what he heard through the stone was water moving in a current, steady and patient, going somewhere it had been going long before the lighthouse was built. He stood, wiped the salt from his hands onto his coat, and went back up the spiral stairs to log the hour, because the light did not tend itself, and because he had learned in three decades that some doors announce themselves only after you have decided not to open them.

after — antemuse material

The keeper shouted into the howling wind, but his words came out twisted and unrecognizable, as if the noise had eaten the meaning before it could escape his lips. He pressed closer, and the door seemed to watch him the way a cell marks its damaged parts for destruction—tagging this impossible intrusion, this broken piece of the cliff, to be swallowed and digested by whatever machinery kept the world whole. He reached for the handle. The metal was warm.

before — baseline

The paper was impossibly crisp, the kind of off-white that doesn't exist anymore, and the ink smelled like a station waiting room — diesel and floor wax and something faintly metallic. Sarah turned it over twice, front and back, as though the other side might explain what the first already had: a single fare, Route 14, Millbrook to Harrowgate, departing 6:15 a.m. tomorrow. The serial number matched her own handwriting, cramped and hurried in the way she used to write things when she was seventeen and late for everything. She hadn't thought about Route 14 in years. She hadn't thought about the morning she'd bought that ticket, the argument still ringing in her ears, the door of the bus hissing shut behind her like a held breath finally released. But here it was, arriving exactly when it was supposed to — just thirty years too late, and one day too early.

after — antemuse material

**Lombard Effect Dynamics applied to the ticket:** She wanted to shout the ticket's warning loud enough to drown out the noise of her life, but the harder she tried to make the date heard, the less sense the message made—volume without clarity. **Selective Autophagy applied to the ticket:** The ticket behaved like a damaged organelle tagged for disposal, marking itself as a broken part of her past that the present needed to engulf and digest before it poisoned the whole system. --- **Next paragraph:** She held the ticket up to the light, and the paper was wrong—too new, too stiff, smelling of a printing press rather than a drawer. The date read tomorrow, and the route number was one the transit authority had decommissioned before she was born. She wanted to call someone, to raise her voice above the noise of the ordinary afternoon and make them understand what had arrived, but the louder she rehearsed the explanation, the more it sounded like nonsense. So she did what the body does with a damaged cell: she tagged the thing as broken, wrapped it in the membrane of her silence, and let the machinery of forgetting begin its work. She placed the ticket inside the envelope again, then inside a book she never opened, then inside the box she kept on the highest shelf. By evening, the ticket had become just another part of her she was quietly digesting, and the tomorrow it promised passed without her boarding.
